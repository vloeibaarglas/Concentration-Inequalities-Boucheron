**Central idea.** For a real symmetric matrix, $\lambda_{\max}(A) = \max_{\|v\|=1} v^T A v$ (Rayleigh quotient), so the largest eigenvalue dominates the quadratic form at *any* fixed unit vector. Test with the uniform vector $v = n^{-1/2}(1,\dots,1)$: $\mathbb{E}[v^T A v]$ is just the average entry of $A$, which is $(n-1)p$.

**Solution.** The adjacency matrix $A$ of $\mathcal{G}(n,p)$ is real symmetric ($X_{i,j} = X_{j,i}$, $X_{i,i} = 0$), with $\mathbb{E}[X_{i,j}] = p$ for $i \neq j$. For a real symmetric matrix, the largest eigenvalue is the maximum of the Rayleigh quotient over unit vectors:
$$\lambda_{\max}(A) = \max_{\|v\| = 1} v^T A v.$$
Hence for every fixed unit vector $v$, $\lambda_{\max}(A) \geq v^T A v$ pointwise, and taking expectations,
$$\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\max}(A)] \geq \mathbb{E}[v^T A v].$$
Take $v = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1,\dots,1)$, so $\|v\| = 1$. Then
$$v^T A v = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^n X_{i,j}.$$
Since the diagonal is identically zero and there are $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal entries, each with expectation $p$,
$$\mathbb{E}[v^T A v] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n(n-1)p = (n-1)p.$$
Therefore $\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\max}(A)] \geq (n-1)p$. ∎
