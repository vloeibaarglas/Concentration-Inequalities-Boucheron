**Central idea.** Follow the two ingredients of the MST argument (Example 3.15), but do not truncate.

1. **Efron–Stein.** Efron–Stein over the $m^2$ independent entries, computed exactly one entry at a time, shows that only the entries used by the optimal permutation $\pi^*$ contribute: $\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)\le\mathbb{E}\sum_{i}X_{i,\pi^*(i)}^2$.
2. **Random-graph step.** This plays the role that connectivity of $G(n,c\log n/n)$ plays for the MST. LP duality gives potentials $a_k-b_l\le X_{kl}$, with equality on $\pi^*$. An expansion property of the random bipartite graphs $\{X_{kl}\le s\}$, valid off an event of probability $O(m^{-2})$, then forces every entry of $\pi^*$ to be at most $T_m=O(\log^2m/m)$.

Hence $\sum_iX_{i,\pi^*(i)}^2\le T_mZ_m$ on the good event. Walkup's classical theorem that $\mathbb{E}Z_m$ is bounded uniformly in $m$ (by $3$), an external result, gives $\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)=O(\log^2m/m)$. Without it, the same argument gives $O(\log^4m/m)$ self-containedly.

The naive transfer of the MST proof, which caps entries at $\delta_m$ and bounds $\mathbb{P}\{Z_m\ne\bar Z_m\}$, does fail; a remark at the end shows this. That failure concerns the truncation device only. The claimed variance bound is true.

**Solution.** Let $X=(X_{kl})_{k,l\le m}$ be i.i.d. Uniform$[0,1]$. Since the entries have a continuous distribution, the optimal permutation $\pi^*$ is a.s. unique. Write $\rho=(\pi^*)^{-1}$.

*Step 1: $\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)\le\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^mX_{i,\pi^*(i)}^2$.*

Fix an entry $(i,j)$. Define two auxiliary optimal costs, neither of which depends on $X_{ij}$:
- $Z^{\rm in}$ is the optimal assignment cost of the $(m-1)\times(m-1)$ minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$;
- $Z^{\rm out}$ is the minimum of $\sum_kX_{k,\pi(k)}$ over permutations with $\pi(i)\ne j$ (with $Z^{\rm out}=+\infty$ if $m=1$).

Then
$$Z_m=\min\big(X_{ij}+Z^{\rm in},\,Z^{\rm out}\big)=Z^{\rm in}+\min(X_{ij},D_{ij}),\qquad D_{ij}:=Z^{\rm out}-Z^{\rm in}.$$
Moreover $(i,j)\in\pi^*$ iff $X_{ij}<D_{ij}$, outside the null event $\{X_{ij}=D_{ij}\}$ (null because $D_{ij}$ is independent of $X_{ij}$).

Let $\mathbb{E}^{(ij)}$ denote integration over $X_{ij}$ only. Then $\mathbb{E}^{(ij)}(Z_m-\mathbb{E}^{(ij)}Z_m)^2=v(D_{ij})$, where $v(d):=\mathrm{Var}(\min(U,d))$ with $U\sim$ Uniform$[0,1]$ and $d$ treated as a constant. Direct computation gives:
- for $d\le0$: $v(d)=0$;
- for $0<d<1$: $\mathbb{E}\min(U,d)=d-d^2/2$ and $\mathbb{E}\min(U,d)^2=d^2-2d^3/3$, so $v(d)=\frac{d^3}{3}-\frac{d^4}{4}$;
- for $d\ge1$: $v(d)=\frac1{12}$.

In every case $v(d)\le\mathbb{E}[U^2\mathbf{1}_{\{U<d\}}]$, which equals $0$, $\frac{d^3}{3}$ and $\frac13$ respectively.

By the Efron–Stein inequality (Theorem 3.1) applied to the $m^2$ independent entries, and by independence of $X_{ij}$ and $D_{ij}$,
$$\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)\le\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\,v(D_{ij})\le\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{ij}^2\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{ij}<D_{ij}\}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\sum_{(i,j)\in\pi^*}X_{ij}^2.\tag{1}$$

*Step 2: LP duality and a monotonicity lemma.*

By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, the assignment LP $\min\{\sum_{k,l}X_{kl}y_{kl}: y\ \text{doubly stochastic}\}$ has optimal value $Z_m$, attained at the permutation matrix of $\pi^*$. LP duality and complementary slackness give reals $a_1,\dots,a_m$ (rows) and $b_1,\dots,b_m$ (columns) with
$$a_k-b_l\le X_{kl}\ \text{ for all }(k,l),\qquad a_k-b_l=X_{kl}\ \text{ if }l=\pi^*(k).$$
(Take $a_k=u_k$ and $b_l=-v_l$ for an optimal dual solution $(u,v)$ of $\max\{\sum u_k+\sum v_l:u_k+v_l\le X_{kl}\}$.)

For $s>0$ let $G_s$ be the bipartite graph with an edge $(k,l)$ iff $X_{kl}\le s$. Write $N_s(S)$ for the neighbourhood in $G_s$ of a set $S$ of rows (a set of columns), and likewise for a set of columns. Put
$$R(\theta)=\{k:a_k\ge\theta\},\qquad C(\varphi)=\{l:b_l\le\varphi\}.$$

*Lemma.* $|R(\theta-s)|\ge|N_s(R(\theta))|$ and $|C(\varphi+s)|\ge|N_s(C(\varphi))|$.

*Proof.* Let $l\in N_s(R(\theta))$, say $X_{kl}\le s$ with $a_k\ge\theta$. Then $b_l\ge a_k-X_{kl}\ge\theta-s$. Also $a_{\rho(l)}=b_l+X_{\rho(l),l}\ge b_l\ge\theta-s$. So the injective map $\rho$ sends $N_s(R(\theta))$ into $R(\theta-s)$.

Symmetrically, let $k\in N_s(C(\varphi))$, say $X_{kl}\le s$ with $b_l\le\varphi$. Then $a_k\le b_l+X_{kl}\le\varphi+s$, and $b_{\pi^*(k)}=a_k-X_{k,\pi^*(k)}\le a_k\le\varphi+s$. So $\pi^*$ maps $N_s(C(\varphi))$ injectively into $C(\varphi+s)$. $\square$

*Step 3: the good event.*

For integers $\sigma\ge1$ put $s_\sigma=12\log(em/\sigma)/m$, which is decreasing in $\sigma$. Define two events:
- $\mathcal{E}_1$: for every integer $1\le\sigma\le m/4$, every set of $\sigma$ rows has at least $2\sigma$ neighbours in $G_{s_\sigma}$, and every set of $\sigma$ columns has at least $2\sigma$ neighbours in $G_{s_\sigma}$.
- $\mathcal{E}_2$: for every set $S$ of more than $m/4$ rows and every set $T$ of more than $m/4$ columns, some $(k,l)\in S\times T$ has $X_{kl}\le32/m$.

*Bound on $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_1^c)$.* Suppose a set $S$ of $\sigma\le m/4$ rows has fewer than $2\sigma$ neighbours. Since $2\sigma\le m$, its neighbourhood lies inside some set $T$ of exactly $2\sigma$ columns. Then all $\sigma(m-2\sigma)\ge\sigma m/2$ entries of $S\times T^c$ exceed $s_\sigma$. If $s_\sigma\ge1$ this is impossible; otherwise it has probability $(1-s_\sigma)^{\sigma(m-2\sigma)}\le e^{-s_\sigma\sigma m/2}=(em/\sigma)^{-6\sigma}$. A union bound, using $\binom m\sigma\le(em/\sigma)^\sigma$ and $\binom m{2\sigma}\le(em/\sigma)^{2\sigma}$ and doubling for columns, gives
$$\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_1^c)\le2\sum_{1\le\sigma\le m/4}\Big(\frac{\sigma}{em}\Big)^{3\sigma}\le2\cdot\frac m4\cdot(em)^{-3}\le\frac{1}{2e^3m^2}.$$
Here we used that $\sigma\mapsto(\sigma/em)^{3\sigma}$ is decreasing on $[1,m]$: the derivative of $3\sigma\log(\sigma/em)$ is $3\log(\sigma/m)\le0$.

*Bound on $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_2^c)$.* If $m\le32$, every entry is $\le1\le32/m$, so $\mathcal{E}_2$ holds surely. For $m>32$ it suffices to consider $|S|=|T|=q:=\lfloor m/4\rfloor+1\ge m/4$. There are at most $4^m$ such pairs, each failing with probability $(1-32/m)^{q^2}\le e^{-(32/m)(m^2/16)}=e^{-2m}$. Hence
$$\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_2^c)\le4^me^{-2m}=e^{-(2-\log4)m}\le e^{-0.6m}.$$

*Step 4: on $\mathcal{E}_1\cap\mathcal{E}_2$, every entry of $\pi^*$ is at most $T_m$.* Put
$$\Delta_m=\frac{12(1+\log_2m)(1+\log m)}{m},\qquad T_m=2\Delta_m+\frac{32}{m}=O\Big(\frac{\log^2m}{m}\Big).$$

Fix $(i,j)$ with $j=\pi^*(i)$. Build a row chain:
- start with $\theta_0=a_i$, so $i\in R(\theta_0)$;
- with $\sigma_k=|R(\theta_k)|$, while $\sigma_k\le m/4$ set $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-s_{\sigma_k}$.

By the Lemma and $\mathcal{E}_1$, $\sigma_{k+1}\ge|N_{s_{\sigma_k}}(R(\theta_k))|\ge2\sigma_k$, so $\sigma_k\ge2^k$. Let $K$ be the first index with $\sigma_K>m/4$. Then $2^{K-1}\le\sigma_{K-1}\le m/4$, so $K\le\log_2m$. Since $s$ is decreasing, each decrement is at most $s_1=12(1+\log m)/m$, hence
$$a_i-\theta_K\le K s_1\le\Delta_m.$$
Therefore $|R(a_i-\Delta_m)|\ge|R(\theta_K)|>m/4$.

The same chain on columns, starting from $b_j$ (so $j\in C(b_j)$) and using the second half of the Lemma, gives $|C(b_j+\Delta_m)|>m/4$.

By $\mathcal{E}_2$ there are $k\in R(a_i-\Delta_m)$ and $l\in C(b_j+\Delta_m)$ with $X_{kl}\le32/m$. Then
$$a_i-\Delta_m\le a_k\le b_l+X_{kl}\le b_j+\Delta_m+\frac{32}{m},$$
so $X_{ij}=a_i-b_j\le T_m$.

*Step 5: conclusion.* On $\mathcal{E}_1\cap\mathcal{E}_2$, each used entry satisfies $X_{ij}^2\le T_mX_{ij}$, so $\sum_{(i,j)\in\pi^*}X_{ij}^2\le T_mZ_m$. On the complement the sum is at most $m$, since there are $m$ entries, each at most $1$. By (1) and Steps 3–4,
$$\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)\le T_m\,\mathbb{E}Z_m+m\Big(\frac{1}{2e^3m^2}+e^{-0.6m}\Big).$$

**External input (cited, not proved here).** Walkup (1979, "On the expected value of a random assignment problem", *SIAM J. Comput.* 8, 440–442) proved that the expected optimal cost is bounded independently of $m$, with bound $3$ (stated in the literature as $\mathbb{E}Z_m<3$, or as $\limsup_m\mathbb{E}Z_m\le3$). Karp (1987) later improved the bound to $2$, and Aldous (2001) proved $\mathbb{E}Z_m\to\pi^2/6$. Only $C:=\sup_m\mathbb{E}Z_m<\infty$ is used; either form of Walkup's bound gives this, since trivially $\mathbb{E}Z_m\le m$ for each fixed $m$. With it,
$$\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)\le C\,T_m+O(m^{-1})=O\Big(\frac{\log^2m}{m}\Big).$$
Without the external bound, Step 4 alone gives $\sum_{\pi^*}X_{ij}^2\le mT_m^2$ on the good event, hence the fully self-contained $\mathrm{Var}(Z_m)=O(\log^4m/m)$.

**Comparison with the MST.** For the MST, connectivity of $G(n,c\log n/n)$ (Exercise 3.12) shows directly that every tree edge is at most $c\log n/n$. The tree analogue of (1) then gives $(n-1)(c\log n/n)^2=O(\log^2n/n)$. Here, connectivity is replaced by the expansion events $\mathcal{E}_1,\mathcal{E}_2$ combined with LP duality.

**Remark (why naive truncation does not transfer).** One might cap the entries, $\bar X_{kl}=\min(X_{kl},\delta)$, and hope that $Z_m=\bar Z_m$ whenever the optimal matching uses only entries at most $\delta$, or whenever $G_\delta$ has a perfect matching. Both implications are false. Take $m=2$, $\delta=1/2$ and
$$X=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.9\\0.1&0.4\end{pmatrix}.$$
Here $Z_2=0.8$, attained by the identity, which uses only entries at most $\delta$. But $\bar X=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.5\\0.1&0.4\end{pmatrix}$ gives $\bar Z_2=\min(0.8,0.6)=0.6<Z_2$. Capping can push a suboptimal matching below the optimum. The argument above avoids this entirely: it never truncates, and bounds the entries of $\pi^*$ itself. This $2\times2$ example is consistent with Step 4. ∎
