**Central idea.** (Barthe's transport proof.) Push the uniform distribution on $(0,1)$ forward to $f/F$ and $g/G$ via the quantile maps $x(t), y(t)$. For a.e. $t$ these maps are differentiable with $f(x(t))\,x'(t) = F$ and $g(y(t))\,y'(t) = G$ (Lebesgue differentiation at the point $x(t)$, legitimate because the image of Lebesgue measure under $x$ is the absolutely continuous measure $f\,du/F$). The interpolant $z(t) = (1-\lambda)x(t)+\lambda y(t)$ satisfies $z' \ge (x')^{1-\lambda}(y')^\lambda$ by weighted AM–GM, so $h(z(t))z'(t) \ge F^{1-\lambda}G^\lambda$ a.e.; the change-of-variables inequality $\int_0^1 h(z)z' \le \int_{\mathbb{R}} h$ for increasing $z$ finishes the proof. No smoothness of $f,g$ and no approximation step is needed. (Note: the hint's second formula should integrate $g$, not $f$.)

**Solution.** Let $F = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f$, $G = \int_{\mathbb{R}} g$. If $F = 0$ or $G = 0$ the right-hand side is $0$ (convention $0\cdot\infty = 0$) and there is nothing to prove.

*Reduction to $0<F,G<\infty$.* For $R>0$ put $f_R = \min(f,R)\,\mathbf{1}_{[-R,R]}$ and $g_R = \min(g,R)\,\mathbf{1}_{[-R,R]}$. Since $0\le f_R\le f$ and $0\le g_R\le g$, we have $f_R(x)^{1-\lambda}g_R(y)^\lambda \le f(x)^{1-\lambda}g(y)^\lambda \le h((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)$, so $(f_R,g_R,h)$ satisfies the hypothesis. Moreover $\int f_R \le 2R^2<\infty$, and $\int f_R\uparrow F$, $\int g_R\uparrow G$ by monotone convergence, so $\int f_R,\int g_R>0$ for $R$ large. If the result holds whenever $0<F,G<\infty$, then $\int h \ge (\int f_R)^{1-\lambda}(\int g_R)^\lambda$ for all large $R$, and letting $R\to\infty$ gives the claim. Hence assume $0<F,G<\infty$.

*Reduction to Borel $h$.* $h$ is Lebesgue measurable, so there is a Borel $h_1$ with $h_1 = h$ off a Lebesgue-null set $E$, and a Borel null set $N\supseteq E$. Put $\tilde h = h_1$ on $N^c$ and $\tilde h = +\infty$ on $N$. Then $\tilde h$ is Borel, $\tilde h \ge h$ everywhere (so the hypothesis holds with $\tilde h$ in place of $h$), and $\int \tilde h = \int h$. It therefore suffices to prove $\int\tilde h \ge F^{1-\lambda}G^\lambda$; we write $h$ for $\tilde h$ from now on.

*Transport maps.* Let $\Phi(u) = \int_{-\infty}^u f$, a continuous nondecreasing function with $\Phi(-\infty)=0$ and $\Phi(+\infty)=F$. For $t\in(0,1)$ let
$$x(t) = \inf\{u : \Phi(u)\ge tF\}, \qquad y(t) = \inf\Big\{u : \int_{-\infty}^u g \ge tG\Big\},$$
so that $\int_{-\infty}^{x(t)} f = tF$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{y(t)} g = tG$ (the second defining integral is over $g$, correcting the hint's typo). Since $tF\in(0,F)$, $x(t)$ is finite; as $\{u:\Phi(u)\ge tF\}$ is a closed up-ray (continuity and monotonicity of $\Phi$), it equals $[x(t),\infty)$. Hence $\Phi(x(t)) = tF$ and
$$x(t)\le u \iff \Phi(u)\ge tF \iff t\le \Phi(u)/F. \tag{1}$$
If $t<s$ then $\Phi(x(t)) = tF < sF = \Phi(x(s))$, so $x(t)<x(s)$: $x$ is strictly increasing. The same holds for $y$.

*Derivatives a.e.* By Lebesgue's theorem the monotone functions $x,y$ are differentiable (with finite derivative) at a.e. $t\in(0,1)$. By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, the set $N_f = \{u : \Phi \text{ is not differentiable at } u \text{ with } \Phi'(u) = f(u)\}$ is Lebesgue-null; let $B\supseteq N_f$ be a Borel null set. By (1), the image of Lebesgue measure on $(0,1)$ under $x$ has distribution function $u\mapsto \Phi(u)/F$, i.e. it is the measure $f(u)\,du/F$. Hence
$$\big|\{t\in(0,1) : x(t)\in N_f\}\big| \le \big|\{t\in(0,1) : x(t)\in B\}\big| = \frac1F\int_B f = 0 .$$
So for a.e. $t$, $x$ is differentiable at $t$ and $\Phi$ is differentiable at $x(t)$ with $\Phi'(x(t)) = f(x(t))$. At such $t$ the one-variable chain rule applied to $\Phi(x(t)) = tF$ gives
$$f(x(t))\,x'(t) = F .$$
In particular $x'(t)>0$ and $f(x(t)) = F/x'(t) >0$. Likewise $g(y(t))\,y'(t) = G$ with $y'(t)>0$ for a.e. $t$. Let $T\subseteq(0,1)$ be the full-measure set on which both identities hold.

*AM–GM.* $z = (1-\lambda)x+\lambda y$ is strictly increasing, and for $t\in T$ it is differentiable with
$$z'(t) = (1-\lambda)x'(t)+\lambda y'(t) \;\ge\; x'(t)^{1-\lambda}y'(t)^\lambda = \frac{F^{1-\lambda}G^\lambda}{f(x(t))^{1-\lambda}g(y(t))^\lambda}$$
by weighted AM–GM. With the hypothesis $h(z(t))\ge f(x(t))^{1-\lambda}g(y(t))^\lambda$ this yields, for every $t\in T$,
$$h(z(t))\,z'(t) \;\ge\; F^{1-\lambda}G^\lambda. \tag{2}$$

*Change of variables for increasing $z$.* We show $\int_0^1 h(z(t))\,z'(t)\,dt \le \int_{\mathbb{R}} h$ for Borel $h\ge0$. Here $z'$ (defined a.e.) is a nonnegative measurable function, being an a.e. limit of difference quotients. For nondecreasing $z$ and $u<v$ in $(0,1)$, $\int_u^v z' \le z(v)-z(u)$ (Fatou's lemma applied to the difference quotients). Define the Borel measure $\mu(B) = \int_{z^{-1}(B)} z'(t)\,dt$ (countably additive since preimages of disjoint sets are disjoint). For $a<b$, $I = z^{-1}((a,b])$ is an interval (as $z$ is monotone) with endpoints $s\le s'$; for $s<u<v<s'$ we have $u,v\in I$, so $\int_u^v z' \le z(v)-z(u)\le b-a$, and letting $u\downarrow s$, $v\uparrow s'$ (monotone convergence) gives $\mu((a,b])\le b-a$. Every open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is a countable disjoint union of intervals of the form $(a,b]$ (each open interval $(\alpha,\beta)$ is the disjoint union of $(c_k,c_{k+1}]$ for a sequence $c_k$ increasing from $\alpha$ to $\beta$, two-sided if $\alpha=-\infty$), so $\mu(O)\le|O|$ for open $O$, and by outer regularity of Lebesgue measure $\mu(B)\le|B|$ for every Borel $B$. Therefore $\int h\,d\mu \le \int h\,dx$ for simple Borel $h\ge 0$ and then, by monotone convergence, for all Borel $h\ge0$; and $\int h\,d\mu = \int_0^1 h(z(t))z'(t)\,dt$ by the definition of $\mu$ (simple $h$ first, then monotone convergence).

*Conclusion.* Combining with (2), and since $T$ has full measure,
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}} h \;\ge\; \int_0^1 h(z(t))\,z'(t)\,dt \;\ge\; \int_0^1 F^{1-\lambda}G^\lambda\,dt \;=\; \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} f\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} g\right)^\lambda.$$
This is the Prékopa–Leindler inequality on $\mathbb{R}$, proved without Brunn–Minkowski. (When $f,g$ are continuous and strictly positive, $x,y$ are $C^1$ by the inverse function theorem, as the hint suggests; the argument above treats general measurable $f,g$ directly, so no approximation of $f,g$ is required.) ∎
