**Central idea.** A repeated eigenvalue of $A$ is a multiple root of its characteristic polynomial $P_A$, so by Exercise 5.25 the discriminant $Q(A) = D(P_A, P_A')$ — a polynomial in the entries — vanishes on $\mathcal{H}_n \setminus \mathcal{H}_n^d$. The coefficients of $P_A$ are real polynomials in the real coordinates of $\mathcal{H}_n \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ (reality follows from $\overline{P_A(\bar\lambda)} = P_A(\lambda)$ for Hermitian $A$), hence so is $Q$; and $Q$ is not identically zero (it is nonzero on $\mathrm{diag}(1,\dots,n)$). By Exercise 5.24 applied to the nonzero polynomial $Q+1$, the zero set $\{Q = 0\} = \{Q+1 = 1\}$ is Lebesgue-null, so $\mathcal{H}_n \setminus \mathcal{H}_n^d$ is null. Since the GUE law has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure on $\mathcal{H}_n$, a GUE matrix has distinct eigenvalues almost surely.

**Solution.** Identify $\mathcal{H}_n$ with $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ via the real diagonal entries and the real and imaginary parts of the strictly upper-triangular entries; Lebesgue measure on $\mathcal{H}_n$ means Lebesgue measure under this identification. The GUE distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to it (independent Gaussian entries), so it suffices to show $\mathcal{H}_n \setminus \mathcal{H}_n^d$ has Lebesgue measure zero.

For $A \in M_n(\mathbb{C})$, let $P_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_k(A)\lambda^k$ with $c_n = 1$. Each $c_k$ is a polynomial with integer coefficients in the entries of $A$. If $A^* = A$, then
$$\overline{P_A(\bar\lambda)} = \overline{\det(A - \bar\lambda I)} = \det(\bar A - \lambda I) = \det(A^T - \lambda I) = P_A(\lambda),$$
using $\bar A = A^T$; hence every $c_k(A)$ is real. Writing $c_k$ as a complex polynomial in the real coordinates of $\mathcal{H}_n$ and splitting into real and imaginary parts, the imaginary part is a real polynomial vanishing on all of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, hence identically zero. So each $c_k$ is a real polynomial in the coordinates.

Let $Q(A) = D(P_A, P_A')$ be the discriminant from Exercise 5.25 (determinant of the Sylvester matrix). It is a polynomial with integer coefficients in the $c_k$, hence a real polynomial in the coordinates of $\mathcal{H}_n$. If $A$ has an eigenvalue of multiplicity exceeding $1$, then $P_A$ has a multiple root, so $Q(A) = 0$ by Exercise 5.25. Thus
$$\mathcal{H}_n \setminus \mathcal{H}_n^d \subseteq \{A \in \mathcal{H}_n : Q(A) = 0\}.$$

$Q$ is not the zero polynomial: for $A_0 = \mathrm{diag}(1, 2, \ldots, n)$, $P_{A_0}$ has the distinct roots $1, \ldots, n$, and the standard product formula $D(P,P') = \pm\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)^2$ for monic $P$ gives $Q(A_0) \neq 0$. Hence $Q + 1$ is a nonzero real polynomial, and
$$\{Q = 0\} = \{Q + 1 = 1\}$$
has Lebesgue measure zero by Exercise 5.24. Therefore $\mathcal{H}_n \setminus \mathcal{H}_n^d$ has Lebesgue measure zero, and a random matrix from the GUE — whose law is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on $\mathcal{H}_n$ — has pairwise distinct eigenvalues almost surely. ∎
