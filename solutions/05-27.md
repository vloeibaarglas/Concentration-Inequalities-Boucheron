**Central idea.** For an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $H$, $A=H-\lambda I$ is singular, so $A\,\mathrm{Adj}(A)=0$ forces every column of $\mathrm{Adj}(A)$ into the one-dimensional eigenspace $\ker A$; but the diagonal entries $\mathrm{Adj}(A)_{jj}=\det(H^{(j)}-\lambda I)$ are nonzero precisely because $\lambda$ is not an eigenvalue of any $H^{(j)}$, so the corresponding eigenvector — and hence every column of $U$ — has all entries nonzero. The hypotheses fail only on zero sets of the discriminant and resultant polynomials from Exercise 5.25, hence on a Lebesgue-null set by Exercise 5.24.

**Solution.** Let $H\in\mathcal{H}_n^d$ share no eigenvalue with any $H^{(k)}$, $1\le k\le n$, and write $H=UDU^*$ with $U\in\mathcal{U}_n$ unitary and $D\in\mathcal{D}_n$ (strictly decreasing diagonal, possible since the eigenvalues are distinct). Fix an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $H$ and let $u$ be the corresponding column of $U$; put $A=H-\lambda I_n$.

Since $\lambda$ is simple, $\ker A=\mathrm{span}\{u\}$ is one-dimensional. As $\det A=0$, the adjugate identity $A\,\mathrm{Adj}(A)=\det(A)I_n=0$ shows each column of $\mathrm{Adj}(A)$ lies in $\ker A$: for every $j$ there is $\alpha_j\in\mathbb{C}$ with $[\mathrm{Adj}(A)]_{\cdot j}=\alpha_j u$. By the hint, $\mathrm{Adj}(A)_{i,j}=(-1)^{i+j}\det(A^{(ji)})$, so
$$\mathrm{Adj}(A)_{j,j}=\det\big(A^{(jj)}\big)=\det\big(H^{(j)}-\lambda I_{n-1}\big)\neq0,$$
the inequality because $\lambda$ is not an eigenvalue of $H^{(j)}$ by hypothesis. Since $\mathrm{Adj}(A)_{j,j}=\alpha_j u_j$, we get $u_j\neq0$ for every $j$. As $\lambda$ was arbitrary, every column of $U$ has all entries nonzero.

It remains that a.e. $H\in\mathcal{H}_n$ satisfies these hypotheses. Identify $\mathcal{H}_n\cong\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ with Lebesgue measure, and let $p_H(z)=\det(H-zI_n)$.

If $H$ has a repeated eigenvalue, $p_H$ and $p_H'$ share a root, so $D(p_H,p_H')=0$ by Exercise 5.25. Now $H\mapsto D(p_H,p_H')$ is a polynomial in the coordinates of $H$ (the coefficients of $p_H$ are polynomial in the entries of $H$, and the discriminant is polynomial in the coefficients), not identically zero: at $H_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,\ldots,n)$, $p_{H_0}$ has $n$ distinct roots, so $D(p_{H_0},p_{H_0}')\neq0$ by the resultant product formula. By Exercise 5.24, $\{H:H\text{ has a repeated eigenvalue}\}$ is Lebesgue-null.

Fix $k$. Then $H$ and $H^{(k)}$ share an eigenvalue iff $p_H$ and $p_{H^{(k)}}$ share a root, iff $\mathrm{Res}(p_H,p_{H^{(k)}})=D(p_H,p_{H^{(k)}})=0$ by Exercise 5.25. This is again a polynomial in the coordinates of $H$, not identically zero: with distinct $d_1,\ldots,d_{n-1}$ and $H$ the arrowhead matrix with $H^{(k)}=D:=\mathrm{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_{n-1})$, $H_{kk}=0$ and $H_{ik}=H_{ki}=1$ for $i\ne k$ (the spike in position $k$). Then $H^{(k)}$ has eigenvalues $\{d_j\}$, while $\det(H-d_jI_n)=-\prod_{i\neq j}(d_i-d_j)\neq0$ for each $j$ (arrowhead determinant, by permutation-similarity to the spike-at-$n$ case), so the resultant is nonzero at $H$. By Exercise 5.24, $\{H:\sigma(H)\cap\sigma(H^{(k)})\neq\emptyset\}$ is null.

Thus a.e. $H\in\mathcal{H}_n$ has distinct eigenvalues and shares no eigenvalue with any $H^{(k)}$. For such $H$ the first part gives $H=UDU^*$ with $D\in\mathcal{D}_n^d$ and $U$ entrywise nonzero; adjusting each column's phase (which preserves $UDU^*$) makes the diagonal entries real positive, i.e. $U\in\mathcal{U}_n^g$ with nonzero entries. With the book's definition of a minor ($U^{(ij)}$: delete row $i$ and column $j$), all minors of $U$ are then invertible: since $U^{-1}=U^*$ and $U^{-1}=\mathrm{Adj}(U)/\det U$, we have $(-1)^{i+j}\det U^{(ij)}=\mathrm{Adj}(U)_{ji}=\det(U)\,\overline{U_{ij}}$, and $|\det U|=1$, so
$$|\det U^{(ij)}|=|U_{ij}|\neq0\qquad(1\le i,j\le n).$$
Hence $U\in\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ and $H\in\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$. So $\mathcal{H}_n\setminus\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$ lies in a finite union of null sets, and it has Lebesgue measure $0$.

*Remark (all square minors).* Inverting $T$ in Exercise 5.28 uses the leading principal blocks of $U$. So there one wants the stronger reading of $\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ used by Anderson–Guionnet–Zeitouni: every square submatrix $U_{IJ}$ ($I,J\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, $|I|=|J|=m$) is invertible. This is still almost sure, and the polynomial method proves it as follows.

*(a) Adjugate as a projector.* Let $H\in\mathcal{H}_n^d$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1>\dots>\lambda_n$ and unit eigenvectors $u_1,\dots,u_n$ (the columns of $U$). Put $c_j=\prod_{l\neq j}(\lambda_l-\lambda_j)\neq0$. Then $\mathrm{Adj}(H-\lambda_jI)=c_j\,u_ju_j^*$. Indeed, $A=H-\lambda_jI$ is Hermitian, so $\mathrm{Adj}(A)$ is Hermitian: $(A^{(ji)})^*=A^{(ij)}$ gives $\det A^{(ji)}=\overline{\det A^{(ij)}}$. By the first part its columns lie in $\ker A=\mathrm{span}\{u_j\}$, so $\mathrm{Adj}(A)=\kappa\,u_ju_j^*$ for some real $\kappa$. Taking traces, $\kappa=\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Adj}(A)=\sum_k\det A^{(k)}$. This is the elementary symmetric function $e_{n-1}$ of the eigenvalues $\lambda_l-\lambda_j$ of $A$. Only the product omitting the zero eigenvalue survives, so $\kappa=c_j$.

*(b) A polynomial detecting the bad set.* Fix $I$ with $|I|=m$. For an $m$-subset $J'\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, put $P_{J'}=\sum_{j\in J'}u_ju_j^*$. Then $(P_{J'})_{II}=U_{IJ'}U_{IJ'}^*$, so $\det(P_{J'})_{II}=|\det U_{IJ'}|^2$. (Changing column phases does not affect this, so the condition $\det U_{IJ'}\neq0$ depends only on $H$.) Treat $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ as indeterminates and define
$$Q^{J'}=\sum_{j\in J'}\Big(\prod_{k\in J'\setminus\{j\}}c_k\Big)\mathrm{Adj}(H-\lambda_jI),\qquad F=\prod_{J'}\det\big(Q^{J'}_{II}\big),$$
with the product over all $m$-subsets $J'$. Each entry of $Q^{J'}$ is a polynomial in the real coordinates of $H$ and in $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ (complex coefficients). A permutation $\sigma$ of the indeterminates sends $c_k\mapsto c_{\sigma(k)}$, hence $Q^{J'}\mapsto Q^{\sigma(J')}$, so it permutes the factors of $F$. Thus $F$ is symmetric in $\lambda$. By the fundamental theorem on symmetric polynomials (valid over any commutative ring), $F=G(e_1(\lambda),\dots,e_n(\lambda))$ for a polynomial $G$ whose coefficients are polynomials in the coordinates of $H$. Substitute the true eigenvalues: $e_k(\lambda(H))$ is, up to sign, a coefficient of $p_H$, hence a polynomial in the coordinates of $H$. So $\mathcal{F}(H):=F|_{\lambda=\lambda(H)}$ is a polynomial in the $n^2$ real coordinates of $H$. On $\mathcal{H}_n^d$, (a) gives $Q^{J'}=(\prod_{k\in J'}c_k)P_{J'}$, and therefore
$$\mathcal{F}(H)=\prod_{J'}\Big(\prod_{k\in J'}c_k\Big)^{m}|\det U_{IJ'}|^2 .$$
So for $H\in\mathcal{H}_n^d$: $\mathcal{F}(H)=0$ iff $\det U_{IJ'}=0$ for some $J'$.

*(c) $\mathcal{F}\not\equiv0$.* Let $A$ be the $m\times n$ real matrix $A_{rb}=b^{\,r-1}$. Its $m\times m$ minors are Vandermonde determinants with distinct nodes, hence nonzero. Gram–Schmidt on the (independent) rows gives $A=LQ$ with $L$ invertible and the rows of $Q$ orthonormal. Then every $m\times m$ minor satisfies $\det Q_{\cdot J'}=\det(A_{\cdot J'})/\det L\neq0$. Complete the rows of $Q$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This gives an orthogonal $W$ whose rows indexed by $I$ are the rows of $Q$, so every $W_{IJ'}$ is invertible. Take $H_0=W\,\mathrm{diag}(n,\dots,1)W^*\in\mathcal{H}_n^d$. Its eigenvector matrix equals $W$ up to column phases, so $\mathcal{F}(H_0)\neq0$. (Checked numerically for $2\le n\le7$, all $m<n$.)

Since $\mathcal{F}\not\equiv0$, its real or imaginary part is a nonzero real polynomial vanishing on $\{\mathcal{F}=0\}$, and Exercise 5.24 makes that set null. There are finitely many $I$ and $J$, and $\mathcal{H}_n\setminus\mathcal{H}_n^d$ is null. So for a.e. $H$, every square submatrix of $U$ is invertible. The conclusion $|\mathcal{H}_n\setminus\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}|=0$ therefore holds under either reading of "all minors".
