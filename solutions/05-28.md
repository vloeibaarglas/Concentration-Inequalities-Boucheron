**Central idea.** A matrix $H \in \mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$ has a unique decomposition $H = UDU^*$ with $D \in \mathcal{D}_n^d$ (eigenvalues sorted decreasingly) and $U \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ (eigenvector phases pinned by the real-positive diagonal, possible since the diagonal entries are nonzero). The ratios $t_{ij} = U_{ij}/U_{ii}$ for $i<j$ then determine $U$ completely: reconstructing the rows one by one, orthogonality to the earlier rows yields at step $i$ a linear system whose coefficient matrix is (conjugate to) a leading principal minor of $U$ — hence invertible by the very-generic hypothesis — while $U_{ii}>0$ and unit norm fix the remaining scale. This reconstruction is a smooth inverse to $T$, and since every nonvanishing condition it uses is open, $T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$, so $H \mapsto (D,T(U))$ is a diffeomorphism onto its open image.

**Solution.** We use the definitions on p. 161 of the book: $\mathcal{U}_n^g$ is the set of $n\times n$ unitaries with real positive diagonal entries; $\mathcal{U}_n^{vg} \subset \mathcal{U}_n^g$ is the subset whose minors are all invertible; $\mathcal{D}_n^d$ is the set of real diagonal matrices with strictly decreasing diagonal entries; $\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$ is the set of Hermitian matrices admitting a decomposition $UDU^*$ with $U \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ and $D \in \mathcal{D}_n^d$. Write $t_{ij} = U_{ij}/U_{ii} \in \mathbb{C}$ for $i<j$ (a pair of real numbers), so that $T: \mathcal{U}_n^{vg} \to \mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$; $T$ is smooth since $U_{ii} > 0$.

*Uniqueness of the decomposition.* Let $H = UDU^* = VEV^*$ with $U,V \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ and $D,E \in \mathcal{D}_n^d$. The diagonal entries of $D$ (resp. $E$) are the eigenvalues of $H$ in strictly decreasing order, so $D = E = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ with $\lambda_1 > \cdots > \lambda_n$. Then $(V^*U)D = D(V^*U)$, and since $D$ has distinct diagonal entries, $V^*U$ is diagonal; being unitary it is a diagonal unitary $D'$. From $U = VD'$ we get $D'_{ii} = U_{ii}/V_{ii} > 0$ (a ratio of positive reals), and with $|D'_{ii}| = 1$ this forces $D'_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$, so $U = V$. Hence the decomposition is unique and
$$\Phi: \mathcal{H}_n^{d,g} \to \mathcal{D}_n^d \times \mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}, \qquad \Phi(H) = (D, T(U)),$$
is well defined.

*$T$ is one-to-one on $\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$.* Let $U,V \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ with $T(U) = T(V) =: t$. We show the rows of $U$ are forced by $t$. Write $r_i$ for the $i$-th row; $(r_i)_j = t_{ij}(r_i)_i$ for $j>i$ with $(r_i)_i = U_{ii} > 0$. For $i=1$, $r_1 = U_{11}(1,t_{12},\ldots,t_{1n})$ and $\|r_1\| = 1$ give $U_{11} = (1+\sum_{j=2}^n|t_{1j}|^2)^{-1/2}$, so $r_1$ is determined by $t$. Suppose $r_1,\ldots,r_{i-1}$ are determined and write $r_i = \phi_i(x)$ with $\phi_i(x_1,\ldots,x_i) = (x_1,\ldots,x_i,t_{i,i+1}x_i,\ldots,t_{in}x_i)$ and $x_i = U_{ii} > 0$. Orthogonality $\langle r_i,r_k\rangle = 0$ for $k<i$ reads
$$\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{(r_k)_j}\,x_j + \left(\overline{(r_k)_i} + \sum_{j=i+1}^n t_{ij}\overline{(r_k)_j}\right)x_i = 0,$$
i.e. $Ax' + x_ic = 0$ with $A_{kj} = \overline{(r_k)_j}$ for $1 \le k,j \le i-1$. But $A$ is the conjugate of the leading $(i-1)\times(i-1)$ principal submatrix of $U$, which is invertible because $U \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$. Hence $x' = -x_iA^{-1}c$, i.e. $x = x_iv$ with $v = (-A^{-1}c,1)$. Since $\phi_i(v) \ne 0$ (its $i$-th coordinate is $1$) and $\phi_i(x) = x_i\phi_i(v)$, unit norm and $x_i>0$ force $x_i = \|\phi_i(v)\|^{-1}$ and $x = \|\phi_i(v)\|^{-1}v$. Thus $r_i$ is determined by $t$ and the previous rows. By induction every row is determined by $t$, so $U = V$: $T$ is injective on $\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$.

*$T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$ is open, with smooth inverse.* Fix $t^0 = T(U^0)$, $U^0 \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$. The reconstruction above expresses each row as a rational function of $t$, with denominators the (conjugates of the) leading principal minors of the partially reconstructed matrix and the positive norms $\|\phi_i(v)\|$. At $t^0$ these minors are those of $U^0$, hence nonzero; by continuity they stay nonzero on a neighborhood $\Omega_0$ of $t^0$, on which the reconstruction defines a smooth map $\tilde S$ with $\tilde S(t^0) = U^0$, $\tilde S(t)$ unitary with real positive diagonal entries, and $T(\tilde S(t)) = t$. Since $U^0$ has all minors invertible and there are finitely many minors, shrinking to $\Omega_1 \subset \Omega_0$ we may assume all minors of $\tilde S(t)$ are invertible for $t \in \Omega_1$, i.e. $\tilde S(t) \in \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ and $t = T(\tilde S(t)) \in T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$. Hence every $t^0 \in T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$ has a neighborhood contained in $T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$: the image is open in $\mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$. On $\Omega_1 \subset T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$, $\tilde S$ coincides with the set-theoretic inverse $S$ of $T$ (the preimage in $\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ is unique by injectivity), so $S: T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}) \to \mathcal{U}_n^{vg}$ is smooth near $t^0$; $t^0$ being arbitrary, $S$ is smooth.

*The diffeomorphism.* Put $J(D,t) = S(t)DS(t)^*$ for $(D,t) \in \mathcal{D}_n^d \times T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$. Then $J$ takes values in $\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$, is smooth (as $S$ is), and $\Phi(J(D,t)) = (D,t)$ by uniqueness of the decomposition, while $J(\Phi(H)) = H$ since $S(T(U)) = U$. Thus $\Phi: \mathcal{H}_n^{d,g} \to \mathcal{D}_n^d \times T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$ is bijective with inverse $J$. It remains to see $\Phi$ is smooth. Let $H_0 = U_0D_0U_0^* \in \mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$. For $H$ Hermitian near $H_0$ the eigenvalues stay distinct; let $\lambda_1(H) > \cdots > \lambda_n(H)$ be the decreasing eigenvalues and $P_i(H)$ the spectral projections, both smooth in $H$ by standard perturbation theory for simple eigenvalues (Riesz formula for $P_i$). With $e_i$ the $i$-th standard basis vector, $u_i(H) = P_i(H)e_i/\|P_i(H)e_i\|$ is smooth near $H_0$ since $P_i(H_0)e_i = (U_0)_{ii}u_i^0 \ne 0$, where $u_i^0$ is the $i$-th column of $U_0$ and $(U_0)_{ii} > 0$. Since $(u_i(H))_i = e_i^*P_i(H)e_i/\|P_i(H)e_i\| = \|P_i(H)e_i\| > 0$ is real, the matrix $U(H)$ with columns $u_i(H)$ is exactly the normalized diagonalizing unitary, smooth in $H$, and $\Phi(H) = (\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1(H),\ldots,\lambda_n(H)),\,T(U(H)))$ is smooth near $H_0$. As $H_0$ was arbitrary, $\Phi$ is smooth. (The same local construction shows $\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$ is open in $\mathcal{H}_n$: for $H$ near $H_0$, $U(H)$ is close to $U_0$, so its minors stay invertible and $H \in \mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$.)

Therefore $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism from $\mathcal{H}_n^{d,g}$ onto $\mathcal{D}_n^d \times T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$, an open subset of $\mathcal{D}_n^d \times \mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$ of real dimension $n + n(n-1) = n^2$. This is the argument of Anderson–Guionnet–Zeitouni (2010, Lemma 2.5.5).

*Remark.* The image is $\mathcal{D}_n^d \times T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg})$, and $T(\mathcal{U}_n^{vg}) \subsetneq \mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$ in general: e.g. for $n=2$ the reconstruction gives $U = (1+|t|^2)^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\-\bar t&1\end{pmatrix}$, whose off-diagonal entries vanish iff $t = 0$, so $T(\mathcal{U}_2^{vg}) = \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$. The "diffeomorphism with $\mathcal{D}_n^d \times \mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)}$" in the statement is thus to be understood as a diffeomorphism onto the open image in that ambient space.
