**Central idea.** The GUE argument of Exercises 5.24–5.30 adapts to real symmetric matrices with one decisive change: eigenvectors are real orthogonal, so there are $n(n-1)/2$ real off-diagonal parameters (not $n(n-1)$), and the Jacobian contributes the Vandermonde $\Delta(\lambda)=\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)$ to the first power, not squared. However, the formula as stated is false: with all entries $N(0,1/n)$ the density of $A$ is not orthogonally invariant (at $n=1$ the true eigenvalue law is $N(0,1)$ while the formula predicts an $N(0,1/4)$ shape), so no clean determinantal formula holds; we prove the corrected version for the orthogonally-invariant GOE normalization. (The prefactor $D_n/\sigma^{2n}$ is not an error, since $\sigma=1/\sqrt{4n}$ is fixed by $n$; see the closing remark.)

**Erratum.** The statement is false as printed. If all entries $(A_{ij})_{i\le j}$ have variance $1/n$, the law of $A$ is not orthogonally invariant, and the eigenvalue density is not of the printed form. For $n=1$, $\lambda_1=A_{11}\sim N(0,1)$, but the formula with $\sigma=1/2$ gives a density $\propto e^{-2\lambda_1^2}$, an $N(0,1/4)$ shape that no choice of $D_1$ repairs. Corrected statement (proved below): the formula holds for the invariant normalization, where $A$ has density $\propto\exp(-\mathrm{tr}(A^2)/(2\sigma^2))$, i.e.\ $A_{ii}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $A_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2/2)$ for $i<j$; the density at $\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_n$ is then $C_{n,\sigma}\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\exp(-\sum_i\lambda_i^2/(2\sigma^2))$.

**Solution.** *The stated formula is false.* For $n=1$, $A=[A_{11}]$ with $A_{11}\sim N(0,1)$, so the eigenvalue $\lambda_1=A_{11}$ has density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\lambda_1^2/2}$. The claimed formula gives, with $\sigma=1/2$, a density proportional to $e^{-\lambda_1^2/(2\sigma^2)}=e^{-2\lambda_1^2}$, i.e.\ an $N(0,1/4)$ shape — a different distribution, and no choice of $D_1$ repairs the shape. More generally, the density $\prod_{i\le j}\sqrt{n/(2\pi)}\exp(-nA_{ij}^2/2)=\mathrm{const}\cdot\exp(-\frac{n}{2}\sum_{i\le j}A_{ij}^2)$ is not a function of $\mathrm{tr}(A^2)=\sum_i\lambda_i^2$ (since $\sum_{i\le j}A_{ij}^2=\frac12\mathrm{tr}(A^2)+\frac12\sum_iA_{ii}^2$), hence not orthogonally invariant, so the $O$-integral below does not separate. We prove the corrected statement.

*Corrected Exercise 5.36.* Let $A$ be real symmetric with density proportional to $\exp(-\mathrm{tr}(A^2)/(2\sigma^2))$ with respect to $dA=\prod_{i\le j}dA_{ij}$ (i.e.\ independent $A_{ii}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, $A_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2/2)$ for $i<j$). Then at $\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_n$ the eigenvalue density is $C_{n,\sigma}\,\Delta(\lambda)\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i^2/(2\sigma^2))$.

*Step 1: distinct eigenvalues a.s.* Let $\mathcal{S}_n$ be the real symmetric $n\times n$ matrices and $\mathcal{S}_n^d$ those with pairwise distinct eigenvalues. As in Exercises 5.24–5.26, the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are polynomials in the entries, and by Exercise 5.25 the discriminant $D(P,P')$ is a nonzero polynomial in the entries vanishing exactly when an eigenvalue repeats; by Exercise 5.24 its zero set has Lebesgue measure $0$. Hence $A$ has distinct eigenvalues a.s.

*Step 2: the diffeomorphism (real case of 5.27–5.28).* Let $\mathcal{O}_n^{vg}$ be the orthogonal matrices with positive diagonal entries and invertible minors, $\mathcal{D}_n^d$ the diagonal matrices with decreasing diagonal, and $\mathcal{S}_n^{d,g}$ the symmetric matrices $A=O\Lambda O^T$ with $O\in\mathcal{O}_n^{vg}$, $\Lambda\in\mathcal{D}_n^d$; as in 5.27, $\mathcal{S}_n\setminus\mathcal{S}_n^{d,g}$ has measure $0$. The map
$$A=O\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)O^T\longmapsto\big(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n),\,T(O)\big),\qquad T(O)=\left(\frac{O_{12}}{O_{11}},\dots,\frac{O_{n-1,n}}{O_{n-1,n-1}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n(n-1)/2},$$
is a diffeomorphism $\mathcal{S}_n^{d,g}\to\mathcal{D}_n^d\times T(\mathcal{O}_n^{vg})$ (real case of 5.28; no real/imaginary splitting is needed).

*Step 3: the Jacobian (real case of 5.29–5.30).* Write $A=O\Lambda O^T$; then $O^TdA\,O=[\delta O,\Lambda]+d\Lambda$ with $\delta O=O^TdO$ real skew-symmetric. Hence, with $B_\ell=O^T\frac{\partial A}{\partial p_\ell}O$ as in (5.7) (now real),
$$(O^TdA\,O)_{ij}=(\delta O)_{ij}(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)+\delta_{ij}\,d\lambda_i.$$
The $n(n+1)/2$ independent differentials $(d\lambda_i)_{i\le n}$, $((\delta O)_{ij})_{i<j}$ map block-diagonally to the entries of the symmetric matrix $O^TdA\,O$, with determinant $\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_j-\lambda_i|$. Since $dA\mapsto O^TdA\,O$ is a rotation (Jacobian $1$),
$$dA=\Delta(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\,d\Lambda\,d\mu(O)\qquad\text{on }\{\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_n\},$$
where $d\mu(O)$ depends only on $O$: the Vandermonde appears to the *first* power (not squared as in the GUE case), because a real symmetric matrix has $n(n-1)/2$ independent real off-diagonal entries.

*Step 4: change of variables.* The joint density of $(\Lambda,O)$ is therefore proportional to $\Delta(\lambda)\exp(-\mathrm{tr}(A^2)/(2\sigma^2))\,d\mu(O)\,d\Lambda$. By orthogonal invariance $\mathrm{tr}(A^2)=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^2)=\sum_i\lambda_i^2$, and integrating the $O$-factor gives a finite constant (it is, up to the Jacobian factor $h(p)$, the Haar volume of the compact orthogonal group, whose complement $\mathcal{O}_n\setminus\mathcal{O}_n^{vg}$ has measure $0$). Absorbing constants, the eigenvalue density on $\{\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_n\}$ is $C_{n,\sigma}\,\Delta(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i^2/(2\sigma^2))$ with $\Delta(\lambda)=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)>0$ on this region (this equals $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\det[\lambda_i^{\,j-1}]_{i,j}$).

*Remark (normalization).* Substituting $\lambda_i=\sigma\mu_i$ gives $C_{n,\sigma}^{-1}=\sigma^{n(n+1)/2}I_n$ where $I_n=\int_{\mu_1>\cdots>\mu_n}\Delta(\mu)e^{-\sum\mu_i^2/2}\,d\mu$. So if $\sigma$ is treated as a free scale parameter, the natural form of the prefactor is $D_n/\sigma^{n(n+1)/2}$ with $D_n=1/I_n$ independent of $\sigma$. This is not an erratum in the printed statement: there $\sigma=1/\sqrt{4n}$ is fixed by $n$, so $D_n/\sigma^{2n}$ is merely one constant depending on $n$ and $D_n$ absorbs the difference ($D_n=\sigma^{2n-n(n+1)/2}/I_n$; e.g.\ for $n=1$ the density $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-\lambda_1^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ corresponds to $D_1=\sigma/\sqrt{2\pi}$). ∎
