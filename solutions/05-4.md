**Central idea.** By subadditivity of entropy (Theorem 4.10) it suffices to prove the one-dimensional inequality. For strictly positive $f$ (reduction via $|f|$ and $f+\varepsilon$), the duality formula (Theorem 4.13) writes the one-dimensional entropy as a supremum over the curve $pe^u+qe^v=1$; the desired bound then becomes a uniform $2\times2$ quadratic-form inequality, i.e. a negative-semidefiniteness condition reducing to three elementary estimates verified by calculus. The constant $c(p)$ is best possible, as testing the third estimate at $s=1-p$ forces $c\ge\frac{\log((1-p)/p)}{1-2p}$.

**Solution.** Let $q=1-p$ and assume $0<p\le\frac12$ (the case $p>\frac12$ follows by symmetry since $c(1-p)=c(p)$; $p=\frac12$ gives $c=2$). Write $\mathbb{E}$ for expectation under $\mathbb{P}_p^{\otimes n}$ and $\mathcal{E}(f)=pq\,\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X)-f(\bar X^{(i)}))^2]$ where $\bar X^{(i)}$ flips the $i$-th coordinate.

By Theorem 4.10, $\mathrm{Ent}(f^2)\le\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Ent}^{(i)}(f^2)]$ with $\mathrm{Ent}^{(i)}$ conditional on $X^{(i)}$. It suffices to show, for each $i$,
$$\mathrm{Ent}^{(i)}(f^2)\le c(p)pq\,\mathbb{E}^{(i)}[(f-\bar f^{(i)})^2],\tag{1}$$
for summing then gives $\mathrm{Ent}(f^2)\le c(p)\mathcal{E}(f)$. Since $\mathrm{Ent}$ depends only on $f^2$ and $(|a|-|b|)^2\le(a-b)^2$, replacing $f$ by $|f|$ can only decrease $\mathcal{E}$; approximating $f\ge0$ by $f+\varepsilon>0$ and letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, it suffices to prove (1) for $f>0$.

Fix $i$ and $X^{(i)}$, and write $a=f|_{X_i=1}>0$, $b=f|_{X_i=-1}>0$. Then (1) is the one-dimensional inequality
$$pa^2\log a^2+qb^2\log b^2-(pa^2+qb^2)\log(pa^2+qb^2)\le c(p)pq(a-b)^2\tag{*}$$
for all $a,b>0$. By Theorem 4.13 applied to $Y=f^2\ge0$ on the two-point space,
$$\mathrm{Ent}_p(f^2)=\max\{pa^2u+qb^2v:pe^u+qe^v=1\}.$$
Hence (*) follows if, with $c=c(p)$, for every $(u,v)$ with $pe^u+qe^v=1$ and every $a,b>0$,
$$pa^2u+qb^2v\le cpq(a-b)^2.\tag{**}$$
For fixed $(u,v)$, (**) is negative-semidefiniteness of $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}p(u-cq)&cpq\\cpq&q(v-cp)\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, i.e.
$$\text{(i) }u\le cq,\qquad\text{(ii) }v\le cp,\qquad\text{(iii) }(cq-u)(cp-v)\ge c^2pq.$$

(i),(ii): On $pe^u+qe^v=1$, $\max u=\log(1/p)$ and $\max v=\log(1/q)$. Put $t=q/p\ge1$, so $c(p)=\frac{(t+1)\log t}{\,t-1\,}$. Then (i) is $\log(1+t)\le\frac{t}{t-1}\log t$, i.e. $N(t):=t\log t-(t-1)\log(t+1)\ge0$. Since $N(1)=0$ and $N'(t)=\frac{2}{t+1}-\log(1+\frac1t)\ge0$ (as $\frac{2s}{1+s}\ge\log(1+s)$ for $s=\frac1t\in(0,1]$, since $\phi(s)=\frac{2s}{1+s}-\log(1+s)$ satisfies $\phi(0)=0$, $\phi'(s)=\frac{1-s}{(1+s)^2}\ge0$), (i) holds. Similarly (ii) is $\log(1+\frac1t)\le\frac{\log t}{t-1}$, i.e. $\psi(t):=\log t-(t-1)\log(1+\frac1t)\ge0$, with $\psi(1)=0$, $\psi'(t)=\frac{2}{t+1}-\log(1+\frac1t)\ge0$.

(iii): Parametrize $s=pe^u\in(0,1)$, so $u=\log(s/p)$, $v=\log((1-s)/q)$, and set $H(s)=(cq-\log(s/p))(cp-\log((1-s)/q))$; we need $H(s)\ge c^2pq$. Now $H(p)=c^2pq$ and $H'(s)=K(s)/(s(1-s))$ where
$$K(s)=s(cq-\log(s/p))-(1-s)(cp-\log((1-s)/q)).$$
Differentiating,
$$K'(s)=c-2-\log\frac{s(1-s)}{pq},\qquad K''(s)=\frac{2s-1}{s(1-s)},$$
so $K'$ strictly decreases on $(0,\tfrac12]$ and strictly increases on $[\tfrac12,1)$; $K$ has at most one critical point in each, hence at most three distinct zeros in $(0,1)$ by Rolle's theorem. For $p<\tfrac12$ there are three: $K(p)=pcq-qcp=0$; $K(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12\big[c(q-p)-\log(q/p)\big]=0$ since $c(q-p)=\log(q/p)$; and with $L=\log((1-p)/p)$,
$$K(1-p)=(1-p)(cq-L)-p(cp+L)=c(q-p)-L=0.$$
These are therefore all the zeros of $K$ on $(0,1)$. Since $\lim_{s\downarrow0}K(s)=-(cp+\log q)<0$ (as $cp+\log q\ge2p+\log(1-p)>0$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$) and $K$ has no zero in $(0,p)$, $K<0$ there. Moreover $K'(p)=K'(1-p)=c-2>0$ for $p<\tfrac12$ (as $c(p)=\frac{\log(q/p)}{q-p}>2$, from $\log t>\frac{2(t-1)}{t+1}$ for $t=q/p>1$), so $K>0$ on $(p,\tfrac12)$ and on $(1-p,1)$, and $K<0$ on $(\tfrac12,1-p)$. Thus $H$ strictly decreases on $(0,p)$, strictly increases on $(p,\tfrac12)$, strictly decreases on $(\tfrac12,1-p)$, and strictly increases on $(1-p,1)$; its minima on $(0,1)$ are at $p$ and $1-p$, where $H(p)=c^2pq$ and $H(1-p)=(cq-L)(cp+L)=c^2pq+L\big[c(q-p)-L\big]=c^2pq$. Hence $H(s)\ge c^2pq$ on $(0,1)$, which is (iii). For $p=\tfrac12$ ($c=2$), $K'(s)=-\log(4s(1-s))\ge0$ with equality only at $s=\tfrac12$, so $K$ is strictly increasing, $K<0$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$ and $K>0$ on $(\tfrac12,1)$; $H$ decreases then increases with minimum $H(\tfrac12)=1=c^2pq$.

Hence (**) holds, proving (*) and therefore Theorem 5.2.

*Optimality.* If (*) holds with constant $c>0$, then by the duality equivalence above, (iii) holds with $c$. Take $s_0=1-p$ (i.e. $u=\log((1-p)/p)$, $v=-\log((1-p)/p)$). Then (iii) gives
$$\big[c(1-p)-L\big]\big[cp+L\big]\ge c^2p(1-p),\qquad L:=\log\frac{1-p}{p}>0,$$
i.e. $cL(1-2p)-L^2\ge0$, so $c\ge\frac{L}{1-2p}=c(p)$. Thus no smaller constant works. ∎
