**Central idea.** If every $i$-section of $B$ is an initial segment, then any "out-of-order" pair $u\notin B$, $v\in B$ with $u$ preceding $v$ cannot share a coordinate (otherwise both lie in a common $i$-section, which would then fail to be initial); so $v=u^c$ is the complement of $u$. Applied to $u=$ the first non-element and $v=$ the last element of $B$, this forces $B=S_{|B|-1}\cup\{v\}$, where $v$ is the immediate successor of $u$ and $v=u^c$. Consecutive complementary pairs are rare: for odd $n$ it is the cross-layer pair (last $k$-set, first $(k+1)$-set), for even $n$ the pair (last $k$-set containing $1$, first $k$-set not containing $1$). These give exactly the two sets of the statement. A direct neighbourhood count shows they have vertex boundary at least that of $S_{|B|}$ — strictly larger for $n\ge3$, but equal for $n\le2$ (so the book's "larger" needs $n\ge3$) — which completes Harper's theorem.

**Solution.** *Conventions.* Identify $x\in\{-1,1\}^n$ with the set $\{i:x_i=1\}\subseteq[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$; then $\|x\|=|x|$, and the complement $x^c=[n]\setminus x$ corresponds to $-x$. Write $L_k=\{x:|x|=k\}$ and $x\prec y$ for the simplicial order: $|x|<|y|$, or $|x|=|y|$ and $\min(x\triangle y)\in x$ (this is the book's rule "$x_i=1$, $y_i=-1$ at the first $i$ with $x_i\ne y_i$"). It is a total order, and $S_N$ is the set of its first $N$ elements. Let $N[A]=A\cup\partial_V(A)$ be the closed neighbourhood, so $|\partial_V(A)|=|N[A]|-|A|$. For $\sigma\in\{-1,1\}$ let $F_i^\sigma=\{x:x_i=\sigma\}$.

*Induced order.* For $x,y\in F_i^\sigma$, the weight comparison is unaffected by the common coordinate $i$, and $\min(x\triangle y)\ne i$. Hence the simplicial order of the vectors with $i$-th coordinate $\sigma$ (used to define $S^{(i,\pm)}_N$ in Exercise 7.11) is the restriction of $\prec$ to $F_i^\sigma$. Thus $C_i(B)=B$ means: for each $\sigma$, $B\cap F_i^\sigma$ is an initial segment of $(F_i^\sigma,\prec)$.

Assume $C_i(B)=B$ for all $i$, put $N=|B|$, and suppose $B\ne S_N$ (so $0<N<2^n$).

**Step 1 (out-of-order pairs are complementary).** Let $u\notin B$, $v\in B$, $u\prec v$. If $u_i=v_i=\sigma$ for some $i$, then $u,v\in F_i^\sigma$; since $B\cap F_i^\sigma$ is an initial segment of $(F_i^\sigma,\prec)$ containing $v$, it contains $u$, a contradiction. Hence $u$ and $v$ differ in every coordinate: $v=u^c$.

**Step 2 ($B$ is $S_{N-1}$ plus the successor of its $N$-th element).** Let $u=\min_\prec(\{-1,1\}^n\setminus B)$ and $v=\max_\prec B$. Since $B$ is not an initial segment, some non-element precedes some element, so $u\prec v$, and by Step 1, $v=u^c$.
- If $w\notin B$ and $w\prec v$, then by Step 1, $w=v^c=u$. Hence every element preceding $v$ other than $u$ lies in $B$, and (by maximality of $v$) nothing after $v$ lies in $B$: $B=\{w:w\preceq v\}\setminus\{u\}$.
- If $w\in B$ and $u\prec w$, then by Step 1, $w=u^c=v$. Any $w$ with $u\prec w\prec v$ lies in $B$ by the previous point, hence equals $v$, which is absurd. So $v$ is the immediate successor of $u$.

Therefore $u$ is the $N$-th element of the order, $v=u^c$ is the $(N+1)$-st, and
$$B=S_{N-1}\cup\{v\},\qquad S_{N-1}=\{w:w\prec u\}.$$
Also $u\prec u^c$ forces $|u|\le|u^c|=n-|u|$.

**Step 3 (which consecutive pairs are complementary).** Two facts about a level $L_k$ ($0\le k\le n$, all levels nonempty): (a) its last element is $\{n-k+1,\dots,n\}$ and its first is $\{1,\dots,k\}$. Indeed, for $w\in L_k$ with $w\ne T:=\{n-k+1,\dots,n\}$, the elements of $w\setminus T$ are $\le n-k$ and those of $T\setminus w$ are $\ge n-k+1$, and $w\setminus T\ne\emptyset$, so $\min(w\triangle T)\in w$ and $w\prec T$; the first element is handled symmetrically. (b) In $L_k$, every set containing $1$ precedes every set not containing $1$, since the minimum of their symmetric difference is $1$.

*Case $|u|<|v|$.* As $v$ is the immediate successor of $u$ and every level is nonempty, $u$ is the last element of $L_{|u|}$, $v$ is the first element of $L_{|u|+1}$, and $|v|=|u|+1$. With $|v|=n-|u|$ this gives $n=2k+1$, $|u|=k$. By (a), $u=\{k+2,\dots,2k+1\}$, and indeed $u^c=\{1,\dots,k+1\}$ is the first element of $L_{k+1}$. Since $u$ is last in $L_k$, $S_{N-1}=L_{\le k}\setminus\{u\}$, and as $k<n/2<k+1$,
$$B=\{x:\|x\|<n/2\}\setminus\{u\}\cup\{u^c\},$$
where $u_i=1$ iff $i\in\{(n+3)/2,\dots,n\}$ and $u^c_i=1$ iff $i\in\{1,\dots,(n+1)/2\}$. These are the book's $y$ and $z$: this is the stated odd exception. (For even $n$ this case cannot occur.)

*Case $|u|=|v|$.* Then $|u|=n-|u|$, so $n=2k$ with $k\ge1$, and $u,u^c\in L_k$. Since $u\prec u^c$ and $\min(u\triangle u^c)=1$, we get $1\in u$, $1\notin u^c$. By (b) and the fact that $u^c$ is the immediate successor of $u$, $u$ is the last element of $L_k^{(1)}=\{w\in L_k:1\in w\}$ and $u^c$ is the first element of $L_k\setminus L_k^{(1)}$. Comparing $w\in L_k^{(1)}$ through $w\setminus\{1\}$, a $(k-1)$-subset of $\{2,\dots,2k\}$, fact (a) shows the last element of $L_k^{(1)}$ is $\{1,k+2,\dots,2k\}$. Hence $u=\{1,k+2,\dots,2k\}$ and $u^c=\{2,\dots,k+1\}$ (indeed the first $k$-set without $1$). Here $S_{N-1}=L_{<k}\cup(L_k^{(1)}\setminus\{u\})$, so
$$B=\{x:\|x\|<n/2\}\cup\{x:\|x\|=n/2,\ x_1=1\}\setminus\{u\}\cup\{u^c\},$$
with $u_i=1$ iff $i\in\{1,(n/2)+2,\dots,n\}$ and $u^c_i=1$ iff $i\in\{2,\dots,(n/2)+1\}$ — the book's $y$ and $z$: this is the stated even exception. (For odd $n$ this case cannot occur.)

This proves the classification: $B=S_{|B|}$, or $B$ is the exceptional set for the parity of $n$.

*Converse (the exceptional set is indeed compressed).* Let $u\prec v=u^c$ be the consecutive complementary pair found above and $B=\{w:w\prec u\}\cup\{v\}$. Fix $i$ and let $\sigma=v_i$, so $u_i=-\sigma$. In $F_i^\sigma$: the elements preceding $v$ are $\{w:w\prec u\}\cup\{u\}$, and $u\notin F_i^\sigma$, so $B\cap F_i^\sigma=\{w\in F_i^\sigma:w\preceq v\}$, an initial segment. In $F_i^{-\sigma}$: $v\notin F_i^{-\sigma}$, so $B\cap F_i^{-\sigma}=\{w\in F_i^{-\sigma}:w\prec u\}$, an initial segment. Hence $C_i(B)=B$ for all $i$, while $B\ne S_{|B|}$ since it omits $u$. So for every $n\ge1$ there is exactly one compressed set that is not an initial segment. (A brute-force enumeration of all compressed sets for $n=1,\dots,9$, script `external_audit_2026-10-30/compare/repair_checks/check_07_13.py`, confirms exactly one non-initial compressed set per $n$, equal to the book's formula, and confirms Step 1 on every compressed set for $n\le6$.)

**Step 4 (the exceptional set has boundary at least that of $S_{|B|}$).** Let $B$ be the exceptional set, $N=|B|$, $B_0=S_N$. Since $|B|=|B_0|$, it suffices to compare $|N[B]|$ and $|N[B_0]|$.

*Odd $n=2k+1$ with $k\ge1$ (i.e. $n\ge3$).* Here $|B|=|L_{\le k}|$, so $B_0=L_{\le k}$ and $N[B_0]=L_{\le k+1}$. We show $N[B]\supseteq L_{\le k+1}$: $L_{<k}\subseteq B$; every element of $L_k$ other than $u$ is in $B$; $u$ has a down-neighbour in $L_{k-1}\subseteq B$ (as $k\ge1$); and each $w\in L_{k+1}$ has $k+1\ge2$ down-neighbours in $L_k$, at most one of which is $u$, so it has a neighbour in $B$. In addition, $N[B]$ contains the sets $u^c\cup\{j\}$, $j\notin u^c$, which lie in $L_{k+2}$ (they exist because $|u^c|=k+1<n$), hence outside $L_{\le k+1}$. So $|N[B]|>|N[B_0]|$.

*Even $n=2k$ with $k\ge2$ (i.e. $n\ge4$).* Here $B_0=L_{<k}\cup L_k^{(1)}$, and $N[B_0]=L_{\le k}\cup L_{k+1}^{(1)}$: every element of $L_k$ has a down-neighbour in $L_{k-1}$; an element of $L_{k+1}$ is adjacent to $L_k^{(1)}$ iff it contains $1$ (removing an element other than $1$ keeps $1$, while if $1\notin w$ no down-neighbour of $w$ contains $1$); and no element above $L_{k+1}$ is reached. We show $N[B]\supseteq N[B_0]$: $L_{<k}\subseteq B$; every element of $L_k$ is in $B$ or has a down-neighbour in $L_{k-1}\subseteq B$; each $w\in L_{k+1}^{(1)}$ has $k\ge2$ down-neighbours in $L_k^{(1)}$ (remove any of its $k$ elements other than $1$), at most one of which is $u$, and the others lie in $L_k^{(1)}\setminus\{u\}\subseteq B$. In addition, $N[B]$ contains $u^c\cup\{j\}$ for $j\in\{k+2,\dots,2k\}$ (nonempty as $k\ge2$), a $(k+1)$-set not containing $1$, hence not in $N[B_0]$. So $|N[B]|>|N[B_0]|$.

*Small cases — correction to the statement.* For $n\le2$ the exceptional set does **not** have a larger boundary; the boundaries are equal. For $n=1$: $B=\{(1)\}$ and $S_1=\{(-1)\}$, both with $|\partial_V|=1$. For $n=2$: $B=\{(-1,-1),(-1,1)\}$ and $S_2=\{(-1,-1),(1,-1)\}$, both with $|\partial_V|=2$. So the book's "larger boundary" holds exactly for $n\ge3$; for every $n$, however, $|\partial_V(B)|\ge|\partial_V(S_{|B|})|$, which is all that Harper's theorem needs. (Brute force, same script: $|\partial_V(B)|$ vs. $|\partial_V(S_{|B|})|$ for $n=1,\dots,9$ is $1/1$, $2/2$, $4/3$, $7/6$, $12/10$, $22/20$, $38/35$, $73/70$, $130/126$.)

**Conclusion: Harper's theorem.** We prove $|\partial_V(A)|\ge|\partial_V(S_{|A|})|$ for all $A\subseteq\{-1,1\}^n$ by induction on $n$, as set up in Exercise 7.11. For $n=1$, sets of size $0$ or $2$ are initial segments, and every set of size $1$ has a boundary of size $1=|\partial_V(S_1)|$. Assume the theorem in dimension $n-1$. Then Exercise 7.11 gives $|C_i(A')|=|A'|$ and $|\partial_V(C_i(A'))|\le|\partial_V(A')|$ for all $A'\subseteq\{-1,1\}^n$ and all $i$, and Exercise 7.12 shows that iterating compressions from $A$ terminates at a set $B$ with $C_i(B)=B$ for all $i$, $|B|=|A|$ and $|\partial_V(B)|\le|\partial_V(A)|$. By Steps 1–4, either $B=S_{|A|}$ or $|\partial_V(B)|\ge|\partial_V(S_{|A|})|$. Therefore
$$|\partial_V(A)|\ge|\partial_V(B)|\ge|\partial_V(S_{|A|})|,$$
which is Theorem 7.6 in dimension $n$. ∎
