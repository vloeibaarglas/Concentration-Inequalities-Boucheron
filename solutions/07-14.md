**Central idea.** Sion's theorem cannot be applied directly on $\mathcal{M}(A)$, since $A$ is only assumed measurable and $\mathcal{M}(A)$ need not be weakly compact. Following the proof of Lemma 7.10, push measures forward through $\chi(y)=(\mathbf{1}_{\{x_j\ne y_j\}})_{j\le n}$ to the finite-dimensional simplex $\mathcal{M}(\chi(A))$ of probability measures on $\{0,1\}^n$, which is convex and compact; there the objective $(\mu,\alpha)\mapsto\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbb{E}_\mu[\pi_j]$ is bilinear, hence convex-continuous in $\mu$ and concave-continuous in $\alpha$, so Sion's theorem applies, and surjectivity of the pushforward transfers the identity back to $\mathcal{M}(A)$.

**Solution.** For fixed $x\in\mathcal{X}^n$ write
$$F(\nu,\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_j\,\mathbb{E}_\nu\mathbf{1}_{\{x_j\ne Y_j\}},\qquad \nu\in\mathcal{M}(A),\ \ \alpha\in B:=\{\alpha:\|\alpha\|_2\le 1\}.$$
Since $\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}\mathbb{E}_\nu[g(Y)]=\inf_{y\in A}g(y)$ for bounded measurable $g$ (Dirac masses belong to $\mathcal{M}(A)$), the first line of (7.1) is $d_T(x,A)=\sup_{\alpha\in B}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}F(\nu,\alpha)$. We check Sion's conditions for $F$ with the infimum taken over measures.

Let $\chi:A\to\{0,1\}^n$, $\chi(y)=(\mathbf{1}_{\{x_j\ne y_j\}})_{1\le j\le n}$, and let $\pi_j$ be the $j$-th coordinate projection on $\{0,1\}^n$. Then $\mathbb{E}_\nu\mathbf{1}_{\{x_j\ne Y_j\}}=\mathbb{E}_{\chi_*\nu}[\pi_j]$. Put $\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{M}(\chi(A))$, the probability measures on $\{0,1\}^n$ supported on $\chi(A)$, and
$$G(\mu,\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_j\,\mathbb{E}_\mu[\pi_j],\qquad \mu\in\mathcal{K},\ \alpha\in B.$$

(i) $\mathcal{K}$ is convex and compact: identifying $\mathcal{M}(\{0,1\}^n)$ with the probability simplex in $\mathbb{R}^{2^n}$ (Euclidean = weak topology), $\mathcal{K}=\{p:\,p_z=0\ \forall\,z\notin\chi(A)\}$ is a closed, hence compact, convex subset; mixtures of measures supported on $\chi(A)$ remain supported on $\chi(A)$.

(ii) For fixed $\alpha$, $\mu\mapsto G(\mu,\alpha)$ is linear, hence convex, and continuous (each $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\pi_j]$ is a linear functional of the probability vector), hence lower semicontinuous.

(iii) For fixed $\mu$, $\alpha\mapsto G(\mu,\alpha)$ is linear, hence concave, and continuous, hence upper semicontinuous; $B$ is convex.

Thus Sion's minmax theorem applies with the infimum over $\mathcal{K}$:
$$\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}\sup_{\alpha\in B}G(\mu,\alpha)=\sup_{\alpha\in B}\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}G(\mu,\alpha).$$

Finally, for each fixed $\alpha$,
$$\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}F(\nu,\alpha)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}G(\mu,\alpha):$$
indeed $F(\nu,\alpha)=G(\chi_*\nu,\alpha)$ gives $\ge$, and $\chi_*:\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathcal{K}$ is onto — given $\mu$ supported on $\chi(A)$, pick $y(z)\in A$ with $\chi(y(z))=z$ for each $z\in\chi(A)$ and set $\nu=\sum_z\mu(\{z\})\delta_{y(z)}\in\mathcal{M}(A)$, so $\chi_*\nu=\mu$. Hence
$$\sup_{\alpha\in B}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}F(\nu,\alpha)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}\sup_{\alpha\in B}F(\nu,\alpha),$$
which is (7.1). The infimum over the compact $\mathcal{K}$ of the continuous $G(\cdot,\alpha)$ is attained, and the supremum over the compact ball $B$ is attained (the proof exhibits the maximizer $\hat\alpha$ via Cauchy–Schwarz), so the saddle point is achieved.
