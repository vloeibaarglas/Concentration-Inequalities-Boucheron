**Central idea.** $f_{t,\sigma}$ is the Gaussian mollification $f_t*p_\sigma$ of the $1/t$-Lipschitz ramp $f_t=(1-d(\cdot,A)/t)_+$. Convolution with a smooth probability kernel preserves the Lipschitz constant and yields a $C^\infty$ function; moreover $\nabla f_{t,\sigma}=(\nabla f_t)*p_\sigma\to\nabla f_t$ at every Lebesgue point, and the eikonal equation $\|\nabla d\|=1$ a.e. on $\{d>0\}$ turns this into the $1/t$ limit. The book's pointwise claim inside the strip is too strong as stated — an explicit two-ball counterexample gives limit $0$ — but the a.e. version holds, which is all the dominated-convergence step needs.

**Solution.** Let $A\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ be Borel, $t>0$, $d(x)=d(x,A)=\inf_{y\in A}\|x-y\|$. Put $\phi_t(s)=(1-s/t)_+=\max(0,1-s/t)$ and $f_t=\phi_t\circ d$. Then $f_t:\mathbb{R}^n\to[0,1]$, $f_t=1$ on $A$, $f_t=0$ outside $A_t=\{d\le t\}$, and $f_t$ is $1/t$-Lipschitz, as the composition of the $1$-Lipschitz $d$ with the $1/t$-Lipschitz $\phi_t$. For $\sigma>0$ let $p_\sigma$ be the $N(0,\sigma^2I_n)$ density and
$$f_{t,\sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f_t(y)\,p_\sigma(x-y)\,dy=(f_t*p_\sigma)(x)=\mathbb{E}[f_t(x+\sigma Z)],\qquad Z\sim N(0,I_n).$$
Since $p_\sigma$ is a probability density and $0\le f_t\le1$, $f_{t,\sigma}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $[0,1]$.

*Smoothness.* $p_\sigma\in C^\infty$ and $\partial^\alpha p_\sigma\in L^1$ for every multi-index $\alpha$ (each derivative is a polynomial times a Gaussian). For $f_t\in L^\infty$, differentiation under the integral sign is justified by dominated convergence, giving $\partial^\alpha f_{t,\sigma}=f_t*(\partial^\alpha p_\sigma)$, bounded and continuous. Hence $f_{t,\sigma}$ is infinitely many times differentiable.

*$1/t$-Lipschitz.* From the expectation form,
$$|f_{t,\sigma}(x)-f_{t,\sigma}(x')|\le\mathbb{E}|f_t(x+\sigma Z)-f_t(x'+\sigma Z)|\le\frac1t|x-x'|,$$
so $f_{t,\sigma}$ is $1/t$-Lipschitz for every $\sigma>0$ (in particular $\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}\|\le1/t$ everywhere).

*Gradient representation.* Integrating by parts in $y$ (boundary terms vanish since $p_\sigma$ is Schwartz and $f_t$ is bounded and Lipschitz),
$$\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)=\mathbb{E}[(\nabla f_t)(x+\sigma Z)]=((\nabla f_t)*p_\sigma)(x),$$
with $\nabla f_t$ the a.e.-defined Rademacher gradient, $\|\nabla f_t\|\le1/t$ a.e.

*Limits outside the strip.* If $x\in\mathrm{int}(A)$ then $\nabla f_t=0$ a.e. on $\mathrm{int}(A)$, so with $\delta=d(x,A^c)>0$,
$$\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)\|\le\frac1t\mathbb{P}(x+\sigma Z\notin A)\le\frac1t\mathbb{P}(\|Z\|\ge\delta/\sigma)\longrightarrow0.$$
If $d(x)>t$ (i.e. $x$ outside $\overline{A_t}$) then $\nabla f_t=0$ a.e. on $\{d>t\}$, so
$$\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)\|\le\frac1t\mathbb{P}(d(x+\sigma Z)\le t)\longrightarrow0,$$
since $d(x+\sigma Z)\to d(x)>t$ a.s.

*Limit in the strip.* Since $\nabla f_t\in L^\infty$, the approximate identity $(\nabla f_t)*p_\sigma(x)\to\nabla f_t(x)$ at every Lebesgue point of $\nabla f_t$ (split $\int|h(x-\sigma z)-h(x)|p_1(z)\,dz$ at $|z|\le M$ and let $\sigma\to0$, then $M\to\infty$). Now $f_t=\phi_t\circ d$, so at every differentiability point $y$ of $d$ with $0<d(y)<t$, $\nabla f_t(y)=-\frac1t\nabla d(y)$. The eikonal equation holds: if $d$ is differentiable at $y$ with $d(y)>0$, then $\|\nabla d(y)\|=1$. Indeed, take $a_k\in A$ with $|y-a_k|\to d(y)$ and $v_k=(y-a_k)/|y-a_k|\to v$, $\|v\|=1$; for $0<s<|y-a_k|$, $d(y-sv_k)\le|y-a_k|-s$, so $\frac{d(y)-d(y-sv_k)}{s}\ge1-\frac{|y-a_k|-d(y)}{s}$; letting $k\to\infty$ then $s\to0$ gives $\langle\nabla d(y),v\rangle\ge1$, whence $\|\nabla d(y)\|=1$ since $d$ is $1$-Lipschitz. By Rademacher's theorem, $\|\nabla d\|=1$ a.e. on $\{d>0\}$, so $\|\nabla f_t\|=1/t$ a.e. on $\{0<d<t\}$. Consequently $\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)\|\to1/t$ for a.e. $x\in A_t\setminus A$ (and $\to0$ for a.e. $x$ outside), and dominated convergence gives $\mathbb{E}_\gamma\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}\|\to\frac1t\,\gamma_n(\{0<d<t\})$.

*Remark.* The book's claim that $\|\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)\|\to1/t$ at every interior point $x$ of $A_t\setminus A$ is false as stated. Counterexample: $A=B(0,1)\cup B(10e_1,1)$, $x=5e_1$, $t=6$. Then $x$ is interior to $A_t\setminus A$, but reflection symmetry across $\{y_1=5\}$ forces the $e_1$-component of $\mathbb{E}[(\nabla f_t)(x+\sigma Z)]$ to vanish, and rotation symmetry about the $e_1$-axis forces it parallel to $e_1$; hence $\nabla f_{t,\sigma}(x)=0$ for every $\sigma>0$, not tending to $1/t$. The a.e. statement proved above is the correct version and suffices for the proof.
