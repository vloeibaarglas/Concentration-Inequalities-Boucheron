**Central idea.** Take $\mathcal{T} = \{1,2\}$ with deterministic $x_1 = x_2 = (1,-1)$, $\varphi_1(x) = x$, $\varphi_2(x) = -|x|$ (both $1$-Lipschitz with $\varphi_i(0) = 0$), and $\Psi$ the identity. Then $\sup_{s}|\varepsilon_1\varphi_1(x_{1,s}) + \varepsilon_2\varphi_2(x_{2,s})| = 2$ a.s. while $\sup_{s}|\varepsilon_1x_{1,s} + \varepsilon_2x_{2,s}| = |\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2|$ has mean $1$: both sides of the contraction inequality equal $1$, so no constant larger than $1/2$ can work.

**Solution.** The second inequality of Theorem 11.6 states, for $1$-Lipschitz $\varphi_i$ with $\varphi_i(0) = 0$ and convex nondecreasing $\Psi$,
$$\mathbb{E}\,\Psi\!\left(\tfrac12\sup_{s\in\mathcal{T}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i\varphi_i(x_{i,s})\Big|\right) \le \mathbb{E}\,\Psi\!\left(\sup_{s\in\mathcal{T}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_ix_{i,s}\Big|\right).$$
Let $n = 2$, $\mathcal{T} = \{1,2\}$, $x_1 = x_2 = (1,-1)$ (deterministic, in the hint's notation $X_{1,1} = X_{2,1} = 1$, $X_{1,2} = X_{2,2} = -1$), $\varphi_1(x) = x$, $\varphi_2(x) = -|x|$, and $\Psi(x) = x$. Both $\varphi_i$ are $1$-Lipschitz with $\varphi_i(0) = 0$.

*Left-hand side.* For $s = 1$: $\varepsilon_1\varphi_1(1) + \varepsilon_2\varphi_2(1) = \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$. For $s = 2$: $\varepsilon_1\varphi_1(-1) + \varepsilon_2\varphi_2(-1) = -\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2$. Hence
$$\sup_{s\in\{1,2\}}\big|\varepsilon_1\varphi_1(x_{1,s}) + \varepsilon_2\varphi_2(x_{2,s})\big| = \max\big(|\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2|,\,|\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2|\big) = 2 \quad\text{a.s.},$$
since for $\varepsilon_i \in \{-1,1\}$ one of $|\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2|$, $|\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2|$ equals $2$ and the other $0$. Thus the left-hand side equals $\tfrac12 \cdot 2 = 1$.

*Right-hand side.* For $s = 1$: $\varepsilon_1x_{1,1} + \varepsilon_2x_{2,1} = \varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2$. For $s = 2$: $\varepsilon_1x_{1,2} + \varepsilon_2x_{2,2} = -(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2)$. Hence
$$\sup_{s\in\{1,2\}}\big|\varepsilon_1x_{1,s} + \varepsilon_2x_{2,s}\big| = |\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2| = \begin{cases} 2 & \text{with probability } 1/2, \\ 0 & \text{with probability } 1/2, \end{cases}$$
so the right-hand side equals $1$.

Both sides equal $1$: the inequality reads $1 \le 1$, i.e. it is tight. Replacing $1/2$ by any $c > 1/2$ would give $2c > 1$ on the left, violating the inequality. Hence the constant $1/2$ is optimal. ∎
