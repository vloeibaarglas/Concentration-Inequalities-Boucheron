**Central idea.** The stated inequality is false: the fixed point $r_*$ controls only $\psi\circ\rho$, not $\rho$ alone, so the term $\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}\rho(r)$ can be arbitrarily large compared to $r_*$. An explicit sub-linear counterexample ($\psi(s)=\min(s,\sqrt{s})$ in effect, $\rho(r)=100r$) gives $u^2\approx 10200$ while $2(a^2+b^2)r_*^2+2c\approx 86$. Under the additional hypothesis $\psi(t)\ge t$ for all $t\ge 0$ (so that $\rho\le\psi\circ\rho$), both terms are controlled by the key sub-linearity bound $\psi(\rho(r))\le\sqrt{n}\,r_*r$, and AM-GM yields the claimed inequality.

**Solution.** We use the book's definition: $\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is sub-linear if it is nondecreasing, continuous, $\psi(x)/x$ is nonincreasing on $(0,\infty)$, and $\psi(1)\ge 1$ (p. 399). By Exercise 13.41(a), $\phi:=\psi\circ\rho$ is sub-linear.

*The statement is false as stated.* Let $n=1$, $a=b=1$, $c=0$,
$$\psi(s)=\begin{cases}s & 0\le s\le 1\\ \sqrt{s} & s\ge 1\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \rho(r)=100r.$$
Then $\psi$ is nondecreasing, continuous, $\psi(x)/x$ equals $1$ on $(0,1]$ and $x^{-1/2}$ on $[1,\infty)$ (hence nonincreasing), and $\psi(1)=1$; $\rho$ is nondecreasing, continuous, $\rho(x)/x\equiv 100$, and $\rho(1)=100\ge 1$. Both are non-trivial sub-linear functions. Now $\psi(100r)=100r$ for $r\le 0.01$ and $10\sqrt{r}$ for $r\ge 0.01$. The function $r\mapsto \psi(100r)/r^2$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $(0,\infty)$ (it equals $100/r$ on $(0,0.01]$ and $10/r^{3/2}$ on $[0.01,\infty)$, matching at $r=0.01$), tends to $+\infty$ as $r\downarrow 0$ and to $0$ as $r\to\infty$; hence $r^2=\psi(100r)$ has a unique positive solution $r_*=10^{2/3}$ (since $(10^{2/3})^2=10^{4/3}=10\sqrt{10^{2/3}}$), with $r_*^2=10^{4/3}<21.6$. Similarly $r\mapsto (\psi(100r)+100r)/r^2$ is continuous and strictly decreasing with the same limits, so $r^2=\psi(100r)+100r$ has a unique positive solution $u$. Since $\psi\ge 0$, $u^2\ge 100u$, so $u\ge 100$; then $u^2=10\sqrt{u}+100u\ge 100u\ge 10000$. But $2(a^2+b^2)r_*^2+2c=4\cdot 10^{4/3}<4\cdot 21.6=86.4<10000\le u^2$, contradicting the claimed bound. Thus the inequality does not follow from sub-linearity alone; the term $\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}\rho(r)$ is invisible to the fixed point $r_*$.

*Corrected version.* Add the hypothesis $\psi(t)\ge t$ for all $t\ge 0$ (equivalently $\rho\le\psi\circ\rho$). Then $u^2\le 2(a^2+b^2)r_*^2+2c$.

Indeed, $\phi=\psi\circ\rho$ is sub-linear with $\phi(r_*)=\sqrt{n}\,r_*^2$. For every $r>0$,
$$\phi(r)\le \sqrt{n}\,r_*r:$$
indeed $\phi(r)/r$ is nonincreasing; if $0<r\le r_*$, then $\phi(r)\le\phi(r_*)=\sqrt{n}\,r_*^2\le\sqrt{n}\,r_*r$; if $r\ge r_*$, then $\phi(r)/r\le\phi(r_*)/r_*=\sqrt{n}\,r_*$. Since $\psi\ge\mathrm{id}$, $\rho(r)\le\psi(\rho(r))=\phi(r)\le\sqrt{n}\,r_*r$ for all $r>0$. Evaluating the equation for $u$ at $r=u$,
$$u^2=\frac{a}{\sqrt{n}}\phi(u)+\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}\rho(u)+c\le a r_*u+b r_*u+c=(a+b)r_*u+c.$$
By AM-GM, $(a+b)r_*u\le \frac{(a+b)^2r_*^2+u^2}{2}$, and $(a+b)^2\le 2(a^2+b^2)$, so $(a+b)r_*u\le (a^2+b^2)r_*^2+u^2/2$. Hence $u^2\le (a^2+b^2)r_*^2+u^2/2+c$, i.e.
$$u^2\le 2(a^2+b^2)r_*^2+2c,$$
as claimed. (The hypothesis $a\ge 1$ is not needed for this argument.)
